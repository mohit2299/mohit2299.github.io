The problems I work on

Every business that sells physical goods faces the same four questions. No background needed. The demos below let you play with each one.

Idea 1

Size curve

Not every size sells equally. A size curve is the share of sales each size gets, such as 5% in XS, 30% in M and 8% in XL.

Buy equal amounts of every size and you will run out of M while XS sits unsold.

Idea 2

The buy

Before a season, a company decides how many units of each item to order from the factory. That order is called the buy, and it is hard to change once placed.

The skill is splitting one fixed order across sizes and items well.

Idea 3

Weeks of supply

How long the stock on hand will last at the current sales pace. Stock of 600 units selling 50 a week is 12 weeks of supply.

Too many weeks means cash stuck in the back room. Too few means empty shelves.

Idea 4

Demand forecast

An estimate of how many units customers will want next week or next month, with a range to show how sure the estimate is.

Everything above depends on it. Better forecasts mean better buys.

Projects

What I built, and what changed

Each one: the problem, what I did, and what came out of it. Described in general terms, with no internal data.

Matching stock to what each size sells

RSQLExcel
The problem
A company was ordering sizes in proportions that did not match what customers bought, so some sizes ran out while others piled up.
What I did
Built a repeatable review that compares each size's sales, current stock and planned order, flags where they disagree, and recommends fixes. Where possible it moves existing stock before ordering new.
What came out of it
A review process the planning team can rerun each season, and a size plan for the next season.

Finding demand for the largest sizes

SQLAnalysis
The problem
Nobody had a clear view of how much demand existed for the biggest and tallest sizes.
What I did
Measured sales of those sizes by product line and color, and separated the ones worth restocking from the ones that were not.
What came out of it
Ordering decisions for those sizes based on what actually sold.

Ranking old stock by what to clear first

RExcel
The problem
The company knew it had old stock, but there was no agreed order for which items to sell down first.
What I did
Built a tool that scores each item by how old it will be by the time it sells, sorts items into four action groups, and applies planned clearance oldest first. It refreshes from supplier files on a schedule and comes with a runbook.
What came out of it
One shared priority list, and a large block of old inventory worked down over months.

One view for deciding what to reorder

RSQLPower BI
The problem
Planners built reorder lists by hand from five different sources that did not share the same product codes.
What I did
Combined the five sources into one view, set a minimum safety stock level, and separated true excess from the buffer a business needs.
What came out of it
A single consolidated view, and projected year-end inventory below the prior year.

Forecasting online sales

PythonRegressionDatabricks
The problem
Planners needed a daily sales forecast for the app and website that could account for promotions and advertising spend.
What I did
Built a model that forecasts visitors, share who buy and order size separately, then multiplies them. It includes ad spend and shows a likely range. I tested it on months it had not seen, and wrote a handover guide with automatic checks.
What came out of it
On held-out months it beat forecasts based on last year's sales. It also showed where it was weak: it over-forecast recently, and simpler models were hard to beat.

Supply chain optimization

PythonReinforcement learningFolium
The problem
Could a program learn to predict whether a delivery will be on time, and could a planner order a list of cities into a sensible route?
What I did
A reinforcement-learning program (it learns by trial and error) that predicts whether a delivery will be on time, trained on a public supply chain dataset. A second tool takes a list of cities, orders the stops with a simple nearest-next rule, estimates travel days, and draws the route on an interactive map.
What I would do next
The route rule is a shortcut and does not guarantee the shortest path, so I would add a smarter improvement step and real limits such as delivery windows. The learning program is sensitive to settings, and a plain prediction model is the baseline it should be compared against.
